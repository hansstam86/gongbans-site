What every start pack has in common, for founders who have shipped software and never had a product made: validate demand first, then the order of operations, the words, the paperwork, and who to e-mail in Shenzhen.

0 · Read this first

Read this first

You have shipped software. You have never had a product made. This page is the part of every start pack that is the same for all 50 ideas: the order of operations, the words people will use, the documents you will need, and the mistakes that cost software founders their first production run. Read it once end to end, then come back to the section you are in.

Three rules carry through everything below:

  • A gongban is a starting point, not a product. The catalogue boards give you working electronics with published files. Your product is the board plus an enclosure, a power path, firmware, a box and a stack of certificates. Each of those is a project.
  • Every number on this site is an estimate until a supplier writes it in a quote. BOM costs, MOQs, lead times and certification fees in the packs are there so you can plan; the quote replaces them.
  • Check the licence before anything commercial. Boards tagged check-licence come from community projects; some licences forbid commercial use or require you to publish your changes. Read the licence on the board's page, and ask when it is unclear.

Where the people are: every start pack has a Who to contact section (section 5) that names the companies on file in gongban.db for that idea, and a Validate demand section (section 2) that comes first. This page tells you what to do with both.

1 · Week zero · validate demand

Week zero: validate demand

Every idea on this site carries a kill question: one measurable test that, if it fails, tells you to stop before you spend money on hardware. Section 2 of every start pack turns it into a gate with that idea's audience, places to find them, an interview script and a pre-order message. This is the worksheet behind it. The order matters: demand first, then the plan, then the boards, then the factory. The beginner mistake is the reverse, and it is only discovered after the tooling invoice.

1. Define the segment. Write one sentence: who (job title or life situation, country), when (the moment the problem bites), what they do today (the workaround and what it costs). The pack's audience line is the starting point; narrow it until you could list twenty named people or companies. "Households" is not a segment; "Dutch households on a dynamic tariff with a P1 meter reader" is.

2. Talk to 15–20 of them before writing any code or ordering any sample. Fifteen minutes each, problem first, product last (the pack has the script). Ten buyers and ten users when they are different people. Write each one up the same day in their words: the last time it happened, what it cost, what they tried, what they said at the price. Stop early only when the first ten all say the same thing and it is "no".

3. Build the fake door. One landing page, before anything exists: the pitch as the headline, a rendered image or a well-photographed mock-up, the price from the pack, one button. Consumer: a refundable deposit (best) or a waitlist e-mail. Business: "request a pilot" leading to a short form and a letter of intent. No code, no boards. Send it to everyone you interviewed and post the problem question, with the link, where the audience talks (the pack names the places we are confident about). Run it for two to four weeks on cold traffic.

4. Read the signal honestly.

Counts as demandDoes not count
Refundable deposits, signed letters of intent, a paid pilot, a date in the calendar to see the prototype"Sounds cool", "I'd buy that", likes, upvotes, newsletter opens
Problems described unprompted, in detail, with numbersAgreement with your description of the problem
Money already spent on a workaroundNever tried anything, not bothered
Conversion from strangersConversion from friends, family and your own network
A named budget and a named decision-makerAn enthusiastic user who cannot sign

Waitlist e-mails convert to purchases at a small fraction; refundable deposits and LOIs at a large one. Plan on the pessimistic end and treat the pack's pass mark as a floor, not a target. estimate

5. Go / no-go, tied to the kill question. Go when the pack's kill question is met by your own numbers, the 15–20 conversations are written up, and you hold at least one item from the left column per segment you plan to sell to. Change the idea when the problem is real but the price, buyer or form factor is wrong: the interviews say which; rewrite the pitch and run the door again (a week, not a quarter). Stop when the kill question fails on honest numbers or nobody has paid for a workaround. Only on go do the start kit, the samples and the "Who to contact" section apply.

If it passes, keep the deposit list: those people are your first beta units and your first reviews. If it fails, you have lost a few weeks and learned what a hardware company would have taken a year to learn.

2 · Samples

Order the sample boards

The start kit (section 4 of each pack) lists catalogue boards to copy from. Before you design anything, buy them and make the product work on your desk with the real modules, even if it is held together with tape.

  • Where. The board's page in the catalogue links to the publisher's own files. The publisher (M5Stack, Seeed Studio, Espressif and the others named in section 5 of the pack) sells the physical boards through its own shop and through the usual distributors. Three of each board is a good number: one to use, one to break, one to compare against.
  • Open-hardware boards (OSHWHub projects) have no shop behind them. You order the bare PCB and parts from a prototype fab using the published Gerber and BOM files, or you pick the nearest commercial module that does the same job. The pack usually names one.
  • Chinese marketplaces. Many gongban kits exist as finished boards on 1688 and Taobao, the physical kit market the catalogue records as a source. Buying there needs a Chinese address and payment method or a purchasing agent; for a first prototype the publisher's international shop is faster and the parts are traceable.
  • What arrives. Boards, sometimes a cable, rarely documentation. The documentation is on the board's page. Expect one to three weeks including customs for a small parcel into the EU. estimate
  • Import of samples. Low-value parcels still carry import VAT in the EU; the courier collects it on delivery with a handling fee. Keep every invoice: these are development costs and you will need the HS codes later.

Milestone: the product does its one job on your desk with the sample boards. Software founders call this the proof of concept. Hardware people call it the looks-like / works-like prototype, usually two separate objects at this stage.

3 · Prototype → production

From prototype to production, in hardware words

The vocabulary matters because your suppliers will use it, and it maps badly onto software releases.

StageWhat it isSoftware analogyTypical quantity
POC / breadboardSample boards wired together, your firmware, no enclosureSpike / prototype branch1–3
EVT (engineering validation)Your own PCB for the first time, hand-built or small-batch assembled, tested against the specAlpha5–20
DVT (design validation)Near-final design in the real enclosure; this is the build you send to the test lab for certificationBeta20–100
PVT (production validation)Built on the factory's production line with the production tooling and test fixtures; proves the line, not the designRelease candidate100–500
MP (mass production)The real runGA releasethe order

Quantities are the usual ranges, not rules. estimate Each stage ends with a review; a design change after DVT means re-testing, and a change after PVT means re-tooling. "Just one more fix" is how a hardware company misses Christmas.

Two more words you will hear in the first week:

  • DFM (design for manufacturing): the factory's review of your files for things that are hard or expensive to build. Ask for it before you pay for anything; good houses do it free or for a small fee.
  • NRE (non-recurring engineering): one-off costs such as tooling for the enclosure, SMT stencils, test fixtures and programming jigs. A quote without NRE is incomplete.
4 · The manufacturer

Find the manufacturer

There are three kinds of company behind every idea on this site, and the first e-mail to each is different. Section 5 of the pack tells you which exist on file for your idea.

The board publisher (M5Stack, Seeed Studio, Espressif, SIMCom, WPI's application unit). They designed the board and sell it. What they are good for: sample units, volume pricing of the module as-is, sometimes a customised variant (your connector, your enclosure, firmware pre-loaded). What to send them: template A from the pack. What they are not: your contract manufacturer for the whole product.

The solution house / ODM (the directory entries). Shenzhen companies that already make products in your category from their own reference designs. What they are good for: taking your spec and delivering a finished, certified product, often faster and cheaper than you designing it, in exchange for less control and a design you do not own. What to send them: a spec sheet and target quantities; ask what they already have that is close. The directory entries in the packs are category matches, companies on file in gongban.db that make this kind of thing; none of them has seen the idea.

The PCBA / EMS house (Kingbrother and Foxlink are on file; there are thousands). They build your design from your files: PCB fabrication, component sourcing, SMT assembly, test, often box build. What they are good for: everything from EVT onwards once you have your own design. What to send them: template B from the pack, with the files.

How to vet any of them before money moves, in order of cost:

  1. Website and company registration. The Chinese company name (公司名) on the pack page is what you search in the national registry; a trading company and a factory can have the same English name.
  2. A video call, camera on, from the factory floor. Ask to see the SMT line and the test stations. A solution house or trading company will show you an office; that is fine as long as you know which one you are talking to.
  3. A paid sample run first. Ten to fifty units of your design, paid in full, before any production order. You learn their communication, their quality and their invoice all at once. estimate
  4. Payment terms. Thirty percent deposit, seventy percent before shipment is the common first-order structure; pay the balance only after you or a third-party inspector has seen the finished goods. estimate
  5. Third-party inspection for the first production run: an inspector visits the factory before shipment and reports against your checklist. This costs a few hundred euros per visit and is the cheapest insurance in hardware. estimate
  6. Ask who else they build for and whether you can call one customer. A good house will say yes.

Red flags, from twenty years of this: a quote that comes back within an hour with no questions; a unit price far below the others with no explanation; refusing a video call; asking for the full amount up front; "no problem" to every technical question; a different company name on the invoice than on the website.

5 · The first quote

The first quote, and how to read it

Your e-mail (templates in section 5 of the pack) asks for samples, volume pricing, NRE, lead time, certification documents and terms. A real quote answers all six. Read it like this:

  • Unit price at each quantity break. Note the currency and whether it includes components, packaging and testing. A PCBA quote often excludes a few "customer-supplied" parts: ask.
  • MOQ (minimum order quantity). This is negotiable for a first order, especially if you pay a higher unit price. The MOQ of the components matters as much as the factory's: one chip only sold on a reel of 3,000 sets your real minimum.
  • NRE and tooling, itemised. Stencil, fixtures, programming jig; enclosure tooling if they do box build (an injection mould for a simple plastic enclosure runs into the thousands of euros and takes weeks; a 3D-printed or sheet-metal enclosure has almost none). estimate
  • Lead time from "files approved and deposit received" to "ready to ship", and the component lead time separately. Long-lead parts (some radio modules, displays, connectors) decide your schedule more than the assembly does.
  • Incoterm. EXW (you collect at their door), FOB Shenzhen (they deliver to the ship or plane; you pay the rest), DDP your city (they deliver duty paid to you). For a first order DDP by courier is the simplest and worth its premium; see section 7.
  • Validity. Quotes expire, usually in 30 days, because component prices move. estimate

Get three quotes. The spread between them teaches you what is expensive in your design.

6 · Certification · CE / FCC

Certification: CE for Europe, FCC for the US

The single most important thing a software founder gets wrong: CE is not a test you pass; it is a declaration you sign. You, the person putting the product on the EU market, declare that it meets every applicable directive and you hold a technical file that proves it. Test labs produce reports that go into the file; a notified body is only required for some radio cases. The CE mark itself you print on the label.

Which directives apply to the ideas on this site (the pack's certification line names the ones for each idea):

Directive / regulationApplies whenWhat goes in the technical file
RED 2014/53/EU (radio)Anything with WiFi, Bluetooth, LoRa, cellular, Thread, NFCRadio test report (EN 300 220, EN 300 328, EN 301 908 …), EMC (EN 301 489), safety, and since 2025 the EN 18031 cybersecurity series for internet-connected devices
EMC 2014/30/EUEverything electronic without radio (RED covers EMC when there is radio)Emissions and immunity reports (EN 55032 / EN 55035 or the product standard)
LVD 2014/35/EUMains-powered equipment (50–1000 V AC)Safety report, typically EN 62368-1; a USB-powered device with a certified external PSU largely sidesteps this
RoHS 2011/65/EUEverythingSupplier declarations for every part; the factory provides most of them
REACHEverythingSubstance declarations; same route as RoHS
WEEE 2012/19/EUEverythingNot a test: you register as a producer in each country you sell in (Germany: stiftung ear), pay a scheme, and put the crossed-out bin on the label
Battery Regulation 2023/1542Anything with a batteryBattery documentation, labelling, removability rules, UN 38.3 transport test for lithium cells
CRA (Cyber Resilience Act) 2024/2847Anything with a digital connection; reporting duties from 2026, full application 2027Vulnerability handling process, security updates for the support period, documentation
GPSR 2023/988Consumer productsA responsible person in the EU on the label, a risk assessment, traceability

Three ways to make this cheaper, all used in the packs:

  • Use a pre-certified radio module. A WiFi or LoRa module that already carries RED test reports (ask the publisher; the e-mail template does) lets the lab test your product as a host, not as a new radio. This is why the packs favour modules over bare chips.
  • Power from USB or a certified external supply. No mains inside the enclosure means no LVD testing of your own board.
  • No battery, no microphone, no camera unless the idea needs them. Each one adds a regulation.

What a test lab costs depends on the radio count and the product; a connected product with a pre-certified module and USB power is at the low end, a battery-powered multi-radio device at the high end. Get a quote from two accredited labs (there are many in Shenzhen and in every EU country) with your DVT units; budget it in the plan as the pack does, and treat the pack's figure as an estimate. estimate Test with the DVT build, in the real enclosure, with the production firmware. A failed EMC test is usually fixed with a few components and a re-spin, so leave time for one.

United States: FCC. Intentional radiators (anything that transmits) need FCC authorisation: a certification through a Telecommunication Certification Body and an FCC ID on the label, or, if you use a module with its own modular approval, mostly the module's FCC ID plus your host testing. Unintentional radiators (digital devices without radio) need a Supplier's Declaration of Conformity under Part 15. Different reports, same labs. Add UKCA for Great Britain if you sell there; it mirrors CE with its own marking.

Documents you will need to produce for all of this, so start them at EVT: a product spec, block diagram and schematics, the BOM with manufacturer part numbers, the risk assessment, the user manual with the safety and disposal text, the label artwork, and the Declaration of Conformity. The factory will not write these for you.

7 · Import & customs

Import and customs

Your goods are in Shenzhen; your customers are in Europe. Between them is a set of paperwork that is boring and entirely predictable once you have done it once.

  • EORI number. Your business's EU customs identifier; apply once with your national customs authority, free, takes days. You cannot import commercially without it.
  • HS / TARIC code. Every product has a tariff classification. Most of the ideas on this site fall in chapter 85 (electrical machinery) or chapter 90 (measuring instruments); the exact ten-digit TARIC code decides the duty rate and is yours to declare correctly. Look it up in the EU TARIC database, then confirm it with your customs broker; your supplier's suggestion is a starting point, not an answer.
  • Duty. Under the Information Technology Agreement most computing and telecoms electronics enter the EU at 0% duty, but sensors, instruments, lamps, toys and kits can carry a few percent. The TARIC code tells you. estimate: check your code
  • Import VAT. Charged on the customs value plus duty plus freight, at your country's standard rate (Germany 19%, Netherlands 21%, as of writing; check yours). If you are VAT-registered you reclaim it in your return, so it is a cash-flow cost, not a real one.
  • Who clears it. A customs broker or the courier's brokerage does the declaration from your commercial invoice and packing list; for a first order the courier's DDP service bundles all of this. For sea freight you appoint a forwarder who handles it.
  • Paperwork from the factory: commercial invoice (with HS codes, Incoterm, your EORI), packing list, and for lithium batteries the UN 38.3 test summary and the dangerous-goods declaration.
  • Product compliance at the border. Customs can and does check for the CE mark, the EU responsible-person address (GPSR) and WEEE registration. Ship compliant goods or do not ship.
  • Selling to consumers across the EU: the IOSS scheme lets you charge destination-country VAT at checkout for low-value consignments shipped from outside the EU; if you stock in the EU (section 8) ordinary VAT rules apply.
8 · Logistics & fulfilment

Logistics and fulfilment

  • Samples and small batches: courier (DHL / FedEx / UPS). Three to seven days, door to door, DDP available. Priced by weight and volume; fine up to a few cartons. estimate
  • Air freight. A pallet or more, airport to airport plus handling; one to two weeks with clearance. Faster than sea, cheaper than courier per kilo above a certain size.
  • Sea freight. LCL (you share a container) or FCL (your own). Thirty to forty-five days Shenzhen to Rotterdam or Hamburg plus clearance, and the cheapest per unit by far. Plan it: a product that ships by sea needs a six-week buffer in the launch plan. estimate
  • Lithium batteries travel under dangerous-goods rules: fewer carriers, more paperwork, sometimes sea only. Design decision, not a shipping decision.
  • Packaging is part of the product and part of the freight cost. Ask the factory for the master-carton dimensions and weight in the quote; a box that fits 24 units instead of 20 pays for itself.
  • Where the stock lives. Options, in order of effort: your garage; a 3PL (third-party logistics warehouse) in the EU that stores, picks and ships from your web shop orders; Amazon FBA; a distributor who buys the pallet. B2B ideas on this site usually ship from a 3PL or directly from the factory to the customer; consumer ideas usually need the 3PL or FBA.
  • Returns and spares. Reserve a few percent of the first run for replacements and reviewers, and decide before launch who opens the returned units. estimate
9 · Landed-cost worksheet

The landed-cost worksheet

The pack's unit economics use an estimated BOM. Replace it with this once you have a quote. "Landed cost" is what one unit costs sitting on your shelf in Europe, before you spend anything on selling it.

  Factory unit price (PCBA + box build, at your quantity)        ______
+ NRE and tooling ÷ units in this run                            ______
+ Freight ÷ units (courier / air / sea, incl. insurance)         ______
+ Duty  = (unit price + freight share) × duty rate for your code ______
+ Customs brokerage and handling ÷ units                         ______
+ Certification and lab fees ÷ units over the first year         ______
+ Compliance fees ÷ units (WEEE scheme, battery scheme, GPSR rep) ______
+ Yield loss: units built that fail test or arrive damaged, %    ______
= LANDED COST PER UNIT                                           ______

  Import VAT is paid on (unit price + freight + duty) and reclaimed if you are
  VAT-registered: track it as cash flow, not as cost.

Rules of thumb that hold for the ideas here, every one of them an estimate until your quote says otherwise: landed cost lands well above the bare BOM once NRE, freight and certification are spread over a first run of a few hundred units; retail price is a multiple of landed cost, not of BOM; and the first run is never the cheap one. The packs' unit-economics tables assume exactly this, which is why their first-year margins look thin.

10 · Outreach e-mails

The outreach e-mails

Every start pack carries two e-mails in section 5, pre-filled with that idea's boards and pitch, each in English and Chinese:

  • A. To a board publisher: samples, volume pricing, customisation, radio test reports, payment terms.
  • B. To a PCBA / EMS house: fabrication and assembly quote from the open files, NRE, lead time, QC, box build, Incoterms.

How to use them: fill the brackets, attach nothing on the first mail except a link to your files if you have them, send to the sales or "contact us" address on the company's own website, and send both language versions in the same message. Expect replies in one to three working days, often on WeChat or WhatsApp after the first exchange; keep the commercial terms in e-mail. A reply that answers all the numbered points is a company worth a video call.

11 · Glossary

Glossary

Gongban (公板)
A reference design, usually from a chip house or solution house, that many products are built on with little change. The catalogue on this site lists them.
BOM
Bill of materials: every part on the board with its manufacturer part number and quantity. The PCBA house quotes from it.
PCBA / SMT
Printed-circuit-board assembly; surface-mount technology, the machine process that places the components.
Gerber / pick-and-place
The file formats a fab needs: the copper layers and drill (Gerber), and where each part goes (pick-and-place / centroid file).
MOQ
Minimum order quantity, of the finished unit and of each component.
NRE
Non-recurring engineering: one-off costs such as tooling, stencils, fixtures.
DFM
Design-for-manufacturing review by the factory.
EVT / DVT / PVT / MP
Engineering, design and production validation builds, then mass production. See section 3.
ODM / OEM
ODM: they design and build, you brand it. OEM: they build your design. Shenzhen uses both words loosely; ask what is meant.
Incoterms (EXW, FOB, DDP)
Who pays and who is responsible for the goods at each step of the journey. See section 5.
HS / TARIC code
The customs classification that sets duty. See section 7.
EORI
Your EU customs identifier.
CE / DoC / technical file
The EU conformity mark, the Declaration of Conformity you sign, and the evidence folder behind it. See section 6.
RED / EMC / LVD / RoHS / WEEE / CRA
The EU directives and regulations most likely to apply. See section 6.
FCC ID / modular approval
US radio authorisation; a module with modular approval carries its own FCC ID.
UN 38.3
The transport safety test every lithium battery needs before it may fly or sail.
3PL
Third-party logistics warehouse that stores and ships your stock.
Kill question / fake door
The one measurable test in each idea that tells you to stop, and the landing-page test that measures it. See section 1.
